Passive Vacuum Regeneration and Controlled Release for Marine Mammal Tags

Marine mammal tags must remain securely attached long enough to collect behavioral and acoustic data, but they must also release safely at the end of deployment. For Project CETI, I developed and tested early pressure-driven vacuum-regeneration prototypes and compact burn-wire release concepts for these two hardware requirements.

01

The Engineering Challenge

Marine mammal tags collect acoustic and behavioral data without requiring invasive attachment. Suction cups make this possible, but small leaks caused by skin texture, skin shedding, hydrodynamic loading, and animal movement gradually reduce the vacuum and shorten deployment time.

Once data collection is complete, the tag must also detach reliably. The complete system therefore needs to maintain suction during deployment while providing a predictable release mechanism at the end.

Can environmental pressure changes during a whale dive be used to restore suction without consuming the tag's limited battery capacity?

02

Marine Tag System Architecture

The project addressed two complementary functions within a marine tag: extending suction-cup attachment and releasing the tag after data collection.

Vacuum Regeneration

Extend attachment duration

A pressure-responsive pneumatic mechanism uses hydrostatic pressure changes during repeated dives to remove fluid from the suction cup and restore its internal vacuum.

  • Powered by environmental pressure changes
  • No continuously powered vacuum pump
  • Pneumatic chambers create piston motion
  • One-way valves control fluid transfer
  • Repeated dive cycles increase suction

Burn-Wire Release

Controlled end-of-deployment release

A replaceable burn-wire element provides a compact method for disconnecting the tag from the suction-cup attachment system.

  • Compact electrically activated release
  • Operation in saltwater
  • Replaceable sacrificial element
  • Controlled failure location
  • Integration with the existing tag body
Attach tag → Regenerate vacuum → Collect data → Release tag

03

Passive Vacuum Regeneration

Sperm whales repeatedly travel between the ocean surface and deep water. These dives create large external-pressure changes that can be converted into mechanical piston motion.

The early prototypes used linked syringe chambers to investigate how piston orientation, chamber volume, and pressure cycling affected suction-cup behavior.

Pext External hydrostatic pressure

Provides the environmental input that drives piston displacement during descent and ascent.

Vs Pneumatic chamber volume

Influences piston motion and the amount of fluid transferred during each pressure cycle.

ΔPcup Suction-cup pressure difference

Quantifies the regenerated vacuum and resulting attachment performance.

N Number of pressure cycles

Repeated dives allow the system to progressively restore vacuum.

Dive pressure changes → Pistons move → Fluid is transferred → Vacuum is restored

Prototype Development and Pressure Testing

I translated the existing pressure-driven concept into testable syringe-based prototypes and developed a sequence of experiments to compare system configurations under controlled high-pressure cycles.

01.Same-Direction Pistons

Syringe pistons were arranged in the same direction to observe how both chambers responded when external pressure increased and decreased.

02.Opposite-Direction Pistons

The piston orientation was reversed to examine whether the system retained greater suction-cup contact after the pressure cycle.

03.With and Without Regeneration

Paired suction cups were tested under the same pressure history to compare the prototype against a cup without a regeneration system.

04.Chamber-Volume Comparison

Prototypes using 1, 3, and 5 mL syringes were compared. Larger chambers improved retained contact area, with limited additional improvement between the 3 and 5 mL configurations.

Pressure Measurement and Design Comparison

Initial prototype evaluation relied on visible changes in suction-cup contact area. I then integrated a pressure sensor so that the system response could be evaluated quantitatively.

Sensor Integration

Convert voltage into pressure

The pressure sensor was connected to the syringe and suction system through tubing and a valve, allowing pressure changes to be measured before and after cycling.

Compact Burn-Wire Release System

In parallel with the vacuum-regeneration work, I tested and redesigned a burn-wire system intended to release the tag after data collection.

01

02

Saltwater Release Testing

A tag release test was conducted in water with approximately 3% salinity using a 9 V electrical input.

Salinity: approximately 3% Voltage: 9 V Burn time: less than 1 minute
03

Miniaturized Replaceable Design

A smaller plug-and-play version was developed to simplify replacement during expeditions and concentrate failure at the intended burn-wire location.

Compact packaging Replaceable component Field-reliability evaluation

The compact prototype exposed a difference between controlled laboratory behavior and field reliability. This result identified packaging, electrical connections, and environmental robustness as important requirements for the next design iteration.

07

Published System Development

The project later developed from early syringe-based prototypes into a linked-cylinder assembly with a dedicated valve block, pressure logger, theoretical model, and whale-tag integration.

The final published system used pressure changes during simulated dive cycles to operate as a passive diaphragm pump. Experimental results confirmed vacuum regeneration with both air and water as the working fluid.

  • Evaluated controlled-release performance in saltwater conditions.
  • Identified laboratory-to-field reliability limitations for the compact release prototype.
